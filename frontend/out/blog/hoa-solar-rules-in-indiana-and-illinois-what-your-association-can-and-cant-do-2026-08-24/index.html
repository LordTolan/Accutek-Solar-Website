HOA Solar Rules in Indiana and Illinois: What Your Association Can and Can't Do

Two states, two very different laws. What to check before you put panels on a subdivision home.

Max Insolation

Out in the country, the only approval most folks need for solar is a building permit and a utility sign-off. But if your house sits in a subdivision with a homeowners association, there's one more set of rules to check before anything gets bolted down.

Indiana and Illinois handle HOA solar rules very differently. Since we work on both sides of the state line, here's a plain-English rundown of each, current as of August 2026. It's general information, not legal advice. Your HOA's documents and an attorney have the final word.

Indiana: First, Find Out When Your HOA Was Formed

Indiana's HOA solar rules sit in a chapter of the state's homeowners association law. Under the law's applicability section, that law covers HOAs established after June 30, 2009 that can charge mandatory dues. It covers older HOAs only if their members have voted to be governed by it.

That detail decided a case this year. In February 2026, the Indiana Court of Appeals affirmed the dismissal of a Fishers homeowner's lawsuit over a denied yard solar project. His HOA was formed before 2009 and hadn't opted in, so the court held the solar chapter didn't apply. It's a memorandum decision, so it isn't binding precedent, but the reasoning was simple: the solar chapter isn't on the list of provisions that apply to every HOA.

So step one in Indiana is to find out when your HOA was formed and whether it ever opted in. If the solar chapter doesn't apply, your covenants control.

Indiana: What a Covered HOA Can and Can't Do

If the chapter does apply, Indiana Code 32-25.5-3.5-5 limits when an HOA can block a system or make you take one down. Some of the allowed reasons:

  • A court has found the system threatens public health or safety, or violates a law.
  • It's on HOA-owned, HOA-maintained or commonly owned property.
  • It's somewhere other than your roof, another structure the HOA approved, or a fenced yard or patio you own and maintain.
  • A roof system sticks up more than 6 inches, doesn't follow the roof slope with its top edge parallel to the roof line, or has frames, brackets or visible wiring that aren't silver, bronze or black.
  • A yard or patio system is taller than the fence line.
  • It's installed in a way that voids material warranties, or differs significantly from what you showed the HOA.

That fence-line rule matters if you're picturing a yard array. In a covered Indiana HOA, a ground mount has to sit in a fenced yard or patio and stay below the fence.

There's also a second path. Under section 4, if your HOA's documents restrict solar or the board denies you for a reason not on that list, you can petition your neighbors. You give them the site plan, the system color, any screening for a non-roof install, and who's selling and installing it. You need signatures from 65% of members, or the number needed to amend the covenants if that's lower. Once you have them, the board can't deny the request if you followed the steps.

Illinois: An Outright Ban Isn't Allowed

Illinois goes further. Under the Homeowners' Energy Policy Statement Act, an HOA, common interest community or condo association can't adopt a rule or use its power in a way that prohibits, or has the effect of prohibiting, solar.

Associations still get a say on design. Section 20 requires an association to adopt a written energy policy statement within 90 days of a member's request or application. That statement can set location, design and architectural standards. The association can choose how panels are laid out on a given roof face, as long as it doesn't bar any roof face or cut estimated annual production by more than 10%. It can't require your neighbors' approval, require a specific technology like solar shingles, or deny you based on whether you buy, lease or finance.

Timing is spelled out too. Under Section 30, the association must process a solar application within 30 days, or within 120 days if it hasn't adopted a policy statement yet. It can't charge a higher application fee than it does for other property changes. If it misses those deadlines, you can give written notice and 10 business days to fix it, then go ahead with the install.

One note for yard arrays: much of the Illinois law's design language is written around roofs and buildings, so ask for your association's energy policy statement early to see how it handles ground mounts.

Before You Sign Anything

  • Get a copy of your covenants, any architectural guidelines and, in Illinois, the energy policy statement.
  • In Indiana, confirm the date your HOA was formed and whether it opted in to the state HOA law.
  • Put the plan in writing: site plan, colors, mounting and screening.
  • Build to what you submitted. Both states give associations leverage over installs that stray from the approved plan.
