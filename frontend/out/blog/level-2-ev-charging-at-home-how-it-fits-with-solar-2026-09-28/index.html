Level 2 EV Charging at Home: How It Fits With Solar

What a home charger takes from your panel, how much energy an EV adds, and how to line charging up with the sun.

Dirk Photon

Got an electric car or truck in the garage, or one on your wish list? Then it's fair to wonder whether solar and home charging work well together here in the Wabash Valley. They can. But it helps to understand how charging works, what it adds to your usage, and how the timing lines up with the sun.

Level 1 vs. Level 2 at Home

According to the U.S. Department of Energy's Alternative Fuels Data Center, most EV drivers charge overnight at home with either Level 1 or Level 2 equipment:

  • Level 1 is the cordset that comes with the vehicle and plugs into a regular outlet. DOE says many drivers can meet their daily needs this way, as long as there's an outlet on a dedicated branch circuit near where they park.
  • Level 2 runs on 240 volts for faster charging. DOE suggests it for drivers with less regular schedules, longer commutes, or large batteries that need more than overnight to fill.

DOE also notes that outdoor installation is safe with outdoor-rated equipment, even when charging in the rain. That's handy if your parking spot is a driveway or pole barn instead of an attached garage.

What Level 2 Takes From Your Panel

A Level 2 charger is a big, steady load. DOE points out that the National Electrical Code treats EV charging as a continuous load, and that NEC Article 625 covers most of the rules for charging equipment. Installations have to meet state and local codes, and permits may be required.

Here's what that looks like on a real spec sheet. Emporia's Pro EV charger sell sheet (revised January 2026) lists:

  • Up to 48 amps hardwired (11.5 kW at 240 V) on a dedicated 60-amp or larger two-pole breaker.
  • Up to 40 amps on a NEMA 14-50 outlet (9.6 kW at 240 V) on a dedicated 50-amp or larger two-pole breaker.
  • An internal GFCI, with a recommendation to use a non-GFCI panel breaker to avoid nuisance tripping.
  • A Type 4 watertight enclosure for indoor or outdoor use, UL 2594 safety listing and ENERGY STAR certification.

Notice the pattern: a 48-amp charger needs a 60-amp circuit. That lines up with the code treating charging as a continuous load, since a circuit carrying a continuous load is sized with headroom above the load. DOE adds that some homes don't have enough electrical capacity for Level 2, and that an electrician can tell you whether yours does. Emporia's Pro also uses data from a panel-mounted energy monitor to adjust its charge rate based on total home use and your panel's rating.

How Much Energy an EV Adds

DOE's example vehicle uses 27 kWh to travel 100 miles. Using that as an example only, driving 40 miles a day works out to about 10.8 kWh a day, or roughly 324 kWh over a 30-day month.

For scale, EIA's 2024 data puts the average Indiana household at 901 kWh a month. Your vehicle's efficiency and your miles will differ, but an EV can be a big chunk of new usage. If you're sizing solar, it should be part of the conversation from the start, not an afterthought.

Lining Up Charging With the Sun

Solar makes power during the day. Most charging happens overnight. If your vehicle charges after dark, that energy comes from the grid or from a home battery.

If your vehicle is home during daylight, say on a farm, with a home office, or on weekends, smart chargers can help soak up midday production. Emporia's Excess Solar feature (as of August 2026) uses its energy monitor to spot when solar production exceeds household use. It then starts the charger at a minimum setting, increases the charge rate as surplus allows, and backs off when household use climbs. Users can also set the breaker size in the app to cap the charger's draw.

Whatever setup you choose, plan the charger and the solar together: panel capacity, circuit size, where the car parks and when it's home.
